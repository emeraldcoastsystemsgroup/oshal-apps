## The verdict, in one paragraph

**As a product sold in a store, the arithmetic says no.** Placed at the very top of the Halloween animatronics shelf — $399.99, the highest price observed in that category on a real retail surface — the self-contained unit loses $21.37 on every unit before a single dollar of overhead. The engine refuses to name a break-even volume for it, because it loses $21.37 on every unit sold and volume makes that worse. The cheaper customer-supplies-the-projector kit is worse, not better: at the incumbent's observed $99.99 price it loses $67.63 a unit, and at the top of the plain inflatables shelf it loses $46.21.

**Sold direct, one configuration clears — but only at the top of the comparable's own price band.** The comparable used here is a projector-and-screen Halloween bundle, and the identical kit sells at $229.99 on a mass retailer's shelf and $414.99 on a marketplace listing. At the $414.99 end the self-contained unit contributes $59.44 per unit, 14.3% of revenue, and the smallest run that breaks even is 4,875 units against a plan of 5,000 — only 2.5% of the ORDER SIZE can be cut before the landed cost stops working. At the $229.99 end — the price a customer comparing the two surfaces would pay — it loses $94.30 a unit and the engine refuses to name a break-even volume at all. The positive answer is not a property of the product; it is a property of which listing you price against.

That best case is also narrow: it needs 80.9% of a 5,000-unit run to sell inside a six-week window, against a sell-through figure that has no evidence behind it whatsoever. The 750 units that do not sell at the planned rate cost $145,583 net of what the liquidation recovers, and that cost is charged against the profit below.

**And every one of those numbers is provisional.** 98.4% of the landed cost rests on lines no supplier has quoted; 0 of 82 registered assumptions carry a vendor quote. The direction of the finding is robust — the gaps are tens of percent, far wider than any single band's uncertainty — but no absolute figure in this document should be committed against.

Ranked bluntly: **the store shelf is not reachable, direct-at-a-premium is arguable, and the software-only tier carries none of the four costs that kill the other two.**

### The break-even is a cliff, not a slope

That 4,875-unit figure is not a margin crossing. It is the exact run size at which the **purchase** quantity — the run plus its scrap allowance — reaches the 5,000-piece quantity every cost band in the dataset is quoted at. One unit below it, every component reverts to the small-run price the dataset itself puts 25 to 60 percent higher. Shown on `B-04`, Projector - SHORT THROW, the largest purchased line:

**One unit of run quantity is worth $404,264 of net income here.** The textbook fixed-cost-over-contribution formula gives 927 units for the same question, and the engine flags the divergence rather than reporting the tidier number — contribution is not constant in volume when component prices are quoted in steps and ocean freight is sold by the container.

The practical reading: **there is no gentle scaling-down of this plan.** A cautious operator who orders fewer units to reduce risk crosses a price break and loses more money, not less. That is the trap the pilot-volume section below makes concrete.

## The single most likely reason it fails

**The money and the part are two different risks, and the sweep ranks the money first.**

The sensitivity sweep's top driver is `M-LEAD` — Component production lead time — which moves the peak cash requirement by $270,410 across its researched range. That is a schedule-and-cash exposure, not a design one, and it is not the projector. The projector cost band, `B-04`, ranks 3 on the same chart at $183,561.

**The projector is nonetheless where the design can fail outright**, which is a different kind of risk from a number moving: the dataset's own geometry gate says the part in that price band may not exist at all. A standard mini projector of the class priced at $28.00–$85.00 is built around a 1.2–1.5:1 throw ratio, which does not close inside a six-foot envelope at the intended face size. If short throw is only available in the DLP module tier, this line triples and the V-A business case likely fails. See gate TG-1 and risk R-1.

Three further failures are unresolved by any arithmetic in this plan and each of them is a plausible ending on its own:

1. **Moisture on the optics.** OPEN AND UNRESOLVED. The same continuous airflow that solves the thermal gate is the moisture problem: the blower pulls outdoor air, including humidity, dew and blown rain, straight past the optics for six hours. A warm lens in cold humid air fogs; a cooling lens after shutdown condenses. This is the most likely source of field failures and returns and no arithmetic in this file resolves it. No number in this document addresses it.
2. **The software cannot run unattended.** A 24-hour hard session cap means the prop dies mid-party and needs a human at the device. Unacceptable in a retail product. Estimated at 3–8 weeks, low confidence, with no scoping session held.
3. **Nobody has ever heard it.** Far-field speech capture over a continuously-running blower two to four feet away is entirely unmeasured. If it cannot hear a child from the walkway, the headline feature does not work and the product is an expensive light.

## What a first-timer can actually order

Every cost band in the dataset is stated at a 5,000-unit order. A first-time vendor with no production history rarely places one. At the dataset's own pilot volume of 500 units, with the stated 25%–60% small-run cost uplift applied:

The pilot lowers the cheque — $217,016 committed before revenue instead of $1,196,874 — and it raises the price the product would have to fetch to $643.08, which is further above every observed ceiling than the full run was. **Smaller is safer and less viable at the same time**, and that is the trap a seasonal hardgood sets for a first-timer: the order size that makes the risk survivable is the order size at which the unit economics stop working.

No component minimum order quantity is modelled anywhere in this plan, because none has been quoted. At pilot volume a supplier minimum is one of the likeliest sources of overbuy, so the pilot figures above are optimistic rather than conservative.

## The tier that is not a hardware venture at all

The dataset carries a third tier with no bill of materials: a seasonal licence to the face renderer for a customer who already owns a projector. It has no landed cost, no tariff exposure, no certification, no inventory and no seasonal cash trough, so **it is not modelled by this engine at all** — there is nothing for a landed-cost stack to compute. The observed comparable price for a digital Halloween decoration download is $24.99, and it is the only tier whose schedule is not governed by a retailer's 9-to-12-month category review calendar.

That is a real finding rather than a consolation prize: the software this venture would be selling **already exists and runs today**, and the tier that ships it carries none of the four costs that make the other two tiers fail.
