## The marketplace trap this model does charge for

A bulky seasonal item pays the 2.5x Q4 storage rate (1.20 vs 0.48 USD/cu ft/month) during exactly the months it is sitting in the warehouse waiting for October. And inventory that does not clear by 1 November sits at the peak rate through December for a product nobody wants until next August. The engine must charge storage on PEAK inventory in Q4, not on average inventory across the year - averaging hides the entire problem.

The engine charges storage at the rate in force in each month the stock is actually held rather than at an annual average: $1.20 per cubic foot per month in the peak months against $0.48 outside them. On this configuration that is $6.72 per unit.

The dataset also carries a warning worth repeating: a bulky home item at a low price point can lose close to half its revenue to marketplace fees before storage or advertising is counted at all. That is a warning about the value tier specifically, and this model reproduces it — the kit's marketplace contribution is $73.68 per unit at a price solved to clear a 25.0% target, which is a price no observed shelf supports.

**The inbound deadline is not negotiable.** Inventory must be received into the fulfilment network by early September; fourth-quarter inbound restrictions and receiving delays are real, and they sit 21 weeks downstream of a purchase order.

## Why a first-time vendor is not a candidate for a shelf

A first-time vendor with no listing, no certification, no factory audit and no production history is not a candidate for a category review. The realistic sequence is: prove sell-through in CH-1/CH-2 for one season, then pitch with data.

And before any of that there is a bill. Separate from and additive to all federal requirements. Typically includes: a supplier agreement, EDI capability, GS1 UPC/GTIN, insurance certificates naming the retailer, a social-compliance factory audit, packaging and drop-test (ISTA) conformance, carton marking and pallet patterns, and a chargeback schedule for every deviation.

Modelled at $22,500 over a $5,000–$40,000 range, and — this is the part first-time vendors miss — **largely incurred before the first purchase order**. This is the category of cost first-time vendors most reliably omit. It is not a tax on the product; it is the cost of being allowed to sell to the account at all, and it is largely incurred BEFORE the first PO.

So the shelf is gated three ways at once: by the arithmetic (the unit loses money at the top observed price), by the calendar (two seasons out for a product that does not exist), and by the vendor requirements (a setup cost incurred before any revenue, on top of a certification programme). **Any one of those alone would defer it.**

## The sequence the arithmetic implies

Not a recommendation dressed as a conclusion — this falls out of the tables above and is stated so a reader can disagree with the arithmetic rather than with a preference.

**First, the tier with no bill of materials.** No landed cost, no tariff exposure, no certification, no inventory, no seasonal cash trough, and no dependence on a retailer's category-review calendar. Observed comparable price $24.99. It is the only tier that can reach a customer this season, and the software it sells already runs. Its real value is not the revenue — it is that it buys the sell-through and acquisition-cost evidence the entire hardware case is missing, without a manufacturing commitment.

**Second, direct at a premium, and only if the sourcing evidence supports it.** The self-contained unit at the TOP of the observed direct-bundle band, $414.99, is the one configuration in this plan that contributes positively, at $59.44 per unit — and at the bottom of that same band, $229.99 for the identical comparable kit on a mass retailer's shelf, it loses $94.30 a unit. The premium case needs 80.9% of the run to sell inside a six-week window and $1,196,874 of funding, 100.0% of it committed before the first sale.

**Third, and not before two seasons out, the retail shelf** — if by then the cost bands have been replaced by quotes that land near their low corners. At the researched midpoint it is not reachable, and the gap is not a rounding error.

**The value tier is the one to stop working on.** It is the intuitive answer — make it cheaper, sell more — and it is the configuration the arithmetic rejects most firmly: no researched value of acquisition cost, outbound freight or envelope cost turns it contribution-positive at the incumbent's price, and the engine reports that no factory price at all, including zero, reaches the required landed cost.
